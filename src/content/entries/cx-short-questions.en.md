## Principle

In a stakeholder interview, a short question produces a more usable answer than an ambitious one. The size of the question says nothing about the size of the work it reveals.

## Why it matters

An elaborate question loses the room. It is heard less well, understood less well, and answered less seriously, and this gets worse the longer the session runs. A short question is absorbed whatever the hour and whatever its position in the list. What comes back is concrete and situated rather than aspirational. But asking what could change tomorrow is a format, not a promise of a small fix. The shortcut is to the real problem, not to easy work.

## How to apply it

- Keep the highest-value question the shortest one you ask.
- Expect a description of the current state rather than a diagnosis. People report what they do; they do not name the underlying problem.
- Do the structural translation yourself. "We use two tools for the same purpose" is a data-silo finding stated as a daily inconvenience.
- Do not size the work from the size of the question.

## Point of caution

Because the format suggests a quick fix, an answer that turns out to require a substantial project can read as scope creep to others. Say so explicitly when it happens.
